# Personal Compass

- **Status:** active content and editorial compass
- **Snapshot:** August 2026
- **Design counterpart:** [Visual Compass](./VISUAL-COMPASS.md)
- **Implementation plan:** [Roadmap](./ROADMAP.md)

This is not a finished manifesto or a permanent definition of who I am. It is a
record of where I am today. Some ideas will become clearer, some will change,
and some may contradict things I believe later. That change is part of the
point.

This compass guides **what belongs here, what I want the site to reveal, and what
must remain private or unresolved**. It is internal guidance, not public copy by
default. The Visual Compass separately defines how the site looks, feels, and
behaves. The two support one another without becoming the same document.

## Who I am right now

I love creating systems that solve real problems. What interests me is not only
the software, but understanding the world around it: how something works, what
people need, which problems are being misunderstood, and which problems truly
deserve attention.

I am interested in the way we organize, take care of one another, and create
community. I am drawn to the idea that people should have more ownership over
what they create, what they know, and the means they use to produce things
together.

I have hope in people and in society. We can love many things, build many
things, and imagine ways of living that do not exist yet. Human beings are
deeply creative, even when our systems make us forget it.

I am also someone who wants to feel. I want to experience anger, sadness,
happiness, affection, desire, frustration, and surprise instead of distancing
myself from them. I want to remain present in my life and allow experiences to
change me.

## Believing in myself

One of the things I am working on is believing in myself.

That does not mean pretending I know everything, controlling everything, or
proving that I am better than someone else. It means trusting that my ideas,
feelings, history, desires, and ways of creating have value. It means giving
myself permission to try, to be visible, to make mistakes, to change, and to
continue.

I do not want my sense of worth to depend entirely on being validated by
another person. I want to be capable of recognizing myself as a complete
person, just as I want to recognize other people as complete persons.

## Seeing the other

An idea I am exploring through Byung-Chul Han's writing is what it means to see
the other in their true otherness.

The other person is not a mirror I can use to validate myself. They are not an
extension of me, a character in my story, or an object that exists to satisfy a
need. They are an independent person with their own history, fears, desires,
forms of love, contradictions, needs, and ways of receiving love.

To really see someone is to let them remain different from me. It is to meet
them without reducing them to what I expect or want them to be.

Paradoxically, learning to see the other this way also gives me the freedom to
see myself in the same way: as another complete person, with an independent
identity, a history, love, desire, fear, and possibility. I do not have to erase
myself in order to connect with someone, and I do not have to erase their
difference in order to love them.

We all have the right to be seen this way. We also have the right to show
ourselves—to be known without believing that there is something inherently
wrong with being visible, vulnerable, complex, or unfinished. Allowing
ourselves to see and be seen can be a starting point for loving more truthfully.

## Love as a question

Love is not a subject I consider resolved. It is something I am trying to
understand more deeply.

I keep returning to questions such as:

- How can I truly look at another person without forgetting to look at myself?
- How can I connect with someone without losing them inside my expectations?
- How can I connect without losing myself inside the relationship?
- How can I love without turning the other person into a source of validation?
- How can I become available for love by becoming more connected to myself?

For me, love begins with attention: recognizing that another person has an
inner life as real and complicated as mine.

This also requires learning to recognize violence. Violence is not only found
in its most obvious forms. It can appear in small acts, in control, in erasure,
in indifference, and in ways of relating that are presented as love but hurt the
other person. Understanding that distinction matters to me.

## Community, creation, and care

I want to build things that are useful, but usefulness is not only efficiency.
A useful project can help people meet, organize, express their needs, make
decisions, exchange what they can offer, or care for a shared place.

Software can be a form of care when it begins by paying attention. The
reforestation system I worked on, Gotchu, the idea for organizing neighborhoods
in my city, and the possibility of creating something for my theatre community
all belong to this search.

I want my abilities to be something I can share. Sometimes creating a website
or a tool can be a way of saying thank you.

## Change and uncertainty

I do not like the idea that a person must become one fixed, coherent thing. My
ideas are constantly evolving, and I want to preserve that movement.

Life cannot be completely controlled. At any moment, I know more than I have
ever known before, and at the same time I still know almost nothing. I will keep
learning about life, philosophy, love, myself, and other people.

There will be movement upward and downward. I do not want to turn life into an
endless demand to recover, improve, and get back up immediately. Frustration,
rest, confusion, and uncertainty are also part of being alive.

## The Spider-Punk connection

What I value in Spider-Punk is not simply the visual style. It is the attitude:
resistance to rigid labels, skepticism toward systems that define what people
are allowed to be, expressive energy, difference, and constant change.

I do not want to reproduce a character or wear rebellion as decoration. I want
the website to carry some of that freedom—to feel handmade, alive, resistant to
being categorized, and capable of becoming something different over time.

## What is part of my world

My life cannot be reduced to software projects. Right now it also includes:

- reading Erich Fromm and Byung-Chul Han while thinking about love, eros, and
  otherness;
- watching films and paying attention to ordinary life, emotion, memory, and
  the ways people see one another;
- discovering breakfast and Italian food through cooking;
- taking theatre classes, expressing myself, exploring emotions, and learning
  how it feels to become someone else for a moment;
- building software, exploring social ideas, and finding new people and
  communities I can help with what I know.

These things do not need separate identities. Their connection is that they are
all part of my life.

## What I want this website to be

I want the website to feel like a personal home: a place I want to return to and
where I can leave something behind.

It can contain finished projects, unfinished ideas, technical lessons, films,
books, food, theatre, emotions, questions, mistakes, and ordinary moments.
Other people can enter and see a part of me, but the site should not become a
performance created only for their approval.

The first feeling I want it to create is curiosity.

It should be understandable without becoming perfectly ordered. It should
preserve some chaos, weirdness, surprise, and discovery. It should show its
process and allow visitors to notice how it changes through time.

It should not pretend that I have reached a final version of myself.

## What I want the website to reveal

I do not want the site to explain all of me at once. I want it to let someone
notice me through accumulated traces.

That means showing values through real things rather than turning them into
slogans. A project can reveal how I pay attention to people. A film reflection
can reveal how I think about memory or love. A cooking mistake can reveal
curiosity, patience, or frustration. An unfinished note can preserve a question
before I know how to answer it.

The public site should make room for:

- what I build and why I thought it was worth building;
- what I am learning, including misunderstandings and changed opinions;
- what I notice in films, books, food, theatre, my city, and ordinary life;
- the people and communities that affect the way I create;
- process, uncertainty, and work that is not impressive yet;
- joy, affection, anger, confusion, play, and other parts of being alive.

The site does not need to publish everything that is private in this compass.
Privacy is not dishonesty. The goal is to choose honest traces, not to turn my
inner life into content.

## How the personal compass becomes content

### Home

The home page should create curiosity and show movement. It should offer a
small, current selection rather than a complete summary of me.

### About

The About page should be specific and human. It can tell visitors what I care
about, where I am, what I am exploring, and how I approach making things. It
should not read like a résumé, a personality inventory, or a polished manifesto.

### Projects

Project stories should show attention, decisions, difficulty, care, mistakes,
and learning. Technology is part of the story, but it is not the reason the
story matters.

### Journal

The Journal is where different parts of my life can coexist without being
forced into separate brands. Films, books, cooking, theatre, technical notes,
personal thoughts, and ordinary observations are all traces from the same life.

### Archive

Dates, earlier versions, unfinished ideas, and changed opinions should remain
visible when they are useful. The archive is not only a catalog; it is evidence
that a person has moved through time.

## Editorial boundaries

To keep the site personal without making it performative:

- Do not invent confidence, certainty, biography, or opinions to complete a
  layout.
- Do not polish every sentence until it loses my natural voice.
- Do not turn vulnerability into a visual gimmick.
- Do not publish a private detail merely because it would make stronger copy.
- Do not reduce my interests to content categories or my projects to tool lists.
- Do not remove contradiction only to make the archive look coherent.
- Date personal statements when their moment in time matters.

When real information is missing, leave an honest placeholder in the plan and
ask me later. The site should be built around my life, not a fictional version
of it.

## Decision test

The Personal Compass answers:

> Is this true to what I care about, and is it a trace I want to leave?

The Visual Compass answers:

> Does this feel like PABS FILE, and is the expression disciplined, readable,
> and human?

If a visually exciting choice misrepresents me, remove it. If an honest piece
of content needs a calmer presentation, let the content lead. Accessibility and
clarity are not compromises to the identity; they are part of the care behind
it.

## A working internal compass

This is not necessarily public copy or a tagline. It is a sentence to help guide
future decisions:

> A personal home for someone learning how to build, connect, care, believe in
> himself, and change.

When deciding whether something belongs here, I can ask:

> Does this help me express, understand, create, connect, or care?

If it does, it may have a place here.
